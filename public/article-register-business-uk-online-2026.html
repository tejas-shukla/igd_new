How to Register a Business in the UK Online in 2026: Fees, Rules & New Identity Checks Explained

J
James Okafor
Published September 2026 · 9 min read

If you're trying to figure out how to register a business in the UK online in 2026, you've probably already hit a wall of outdated guides still quoting the old £50 incorporation fee. Things have changed significantly — and knowing the real numbers before you start will save you both money and headaches. This guide covers everything: the updated Companies House fees, the new mandatory identity checks under ECCTA, the sole trader versus limited company decision, and the practical steps to get registered correctly from day one.

TL;DR — Key Things to Know Before You Register

  • The Companies House digital incorporation fee doubled to £100 on 1 February 2026 — most older guides still show £50, so don't trust them.
  • New identity verification is now mandatory for all company directors and PSCs under ECCTA — you can't skip it, and existing directors have until 18 November 2026 to comply.
  • Sole trader registration is still free via HMRC, but offers no liability protection — the right structure depends on your risk and tax position.

Sole Trader vs Limited Company UK 2026: Which Structure Is Right for You?

Before you touch a registration form, this is the decision that actually matters. The sole trader vs limited company UK 2026 question comes down to four things: liability, tax, admin burden, and how you want to appear to clients and investors.

As a sole trader, you and the business are legally the same entity. Your personal assets — your savings, your home — are on the line if the business runs into debt or legal trouble. On the upside, registering as a sole trader costs nothing in government fees. You simply notify HMRC that you're self-employed, file a Self Assessment return each year, and pay Income Tax plus Class 4 National Insurance on your profits. Simple. But from 2026, it's getting slightly more complex: Making Tax Digital for Income Tax Self Assessment (MTD for ITSA) is rolling out in phases, starting with sole traders earning above the threshold. This means quarterly digital record-keeping obligations — a real administrative shift worth factoring into your decision.

A limited company is a separate legal entity. Your personal liability is limited to what you've invested. You pay yourself a salary (taxed through PAYE) and dividends (taxed at lower dividend rates), and the company pays Corporation Tax at 19% on profits up to £50,000, or 25% on profits over £250,000 (with marginal relief in between). This often results in a lower overall tax bill once profits are meaningful — typically from around £30,000–£40,000 net profit upward, though it varies by situation. The tradeoff: more paperwork, a Companies House filing requirement, and the new identity verification obligations described below.

Founder rule of thumb: If you're testing an idea with low revenue and low risk, start sole trader and switch later. If you're building something with employees, clients who care about liability, or real profit potential, start limited.

Companies House Registration Fee 2026: The Real Numbers

Here's where most guides from 2024 and early 2025 will mislead you. On 1 February 2026, Companies House updated its entire fee schedule — the first significant change in years. If you're registering a limited company in the UK online in 2026, these are the fees that actually apply:

  • Digital incorporation (standard, software route): £100 — up from £50. This is the route most online formation agents use.
  • Same-day digital incorporation (software route only): £156 — up from £78.
  • Paper incorporation: £124 — up from £71.
  • Annual Confirmation Statement (digital filing): £50 — up from £34. This is a recurring cost every active limited company pays at least once a year.

The Companies House confirmation statement fee 2026 increase is particularly important to budget for, because it's not a one-off — it's an annual obligation. If you're using a formation agent or accountant to file it on your behalf, their service fee comes on top of this.

For most founders registering a limited company UK online, the digital software route via Companies House directly (or through a formation agent) is the fastest and cheapest standard option. Paper is slower and costs more — there's no reason to use it in 2026.

ECCTA Identity Verification: What Every New Director Must Do

This is the part most founders are caught off guard by. Under the Economic Crime and Corporate Transparency Act (ECCTA), mandatory identity verification (IDV) for company directors and Persons with Significant Control (PSCs) became live on 18 November 2025.

Here's what ECCTA identity verification at Companies House means in practice:

  • New incorporations from 18 November 2025 onward: All directors and PSCs must complete identity verification as part of the registration process — you cannot incorporate without it.
  • Existing directors and PSCs: You have a transition period. The deadline to complete IDV is 18 November 2026. Don't leave it to the last minute — Companies House systems are expected to be busy as the deadline approaches.
  • How to verify: The free route is via GOV.UK One Login — you'll need a valid photo ID (passport or UK driving licence) and to complete a face-matching check. Alternatively, you can verify through a registered Authorised Corporate Service Provider (ACSP), such as a solicitor, accountant, or formation agent — this usually comes with a fee but may be more straightforward for non-UK residents or those with older ID documents.

PSCs — those who own more than 25% of shares or voting rights, or otherwise exercise significant control — must verify even if they are not directors. If you're a solo founder owning 100% of your company, you're both the director and the PSC: you verify once, but in both capacities.

Failure to comply will eventually result in annotations on the Companies House register — a visible flag that could affect your credibility with banks and clients. Take this seriously.

UK Business Registration Steps for Beginners: A Clear Walkthrough

Here's the practical step-by-step for registering a limited company online in the UK in 2026 — no jargon, just the sequence.

  • Step 1 — Choose your company name. Check availability on the Companies House name availability tool. Your name must be unique, not offensive, and not too similar to an existing registered company. It must end in "Limited" or "Ltd".
  • Step 2 — Decide your registered office address. This must be a physical UK address in the jurisdiction of incorporation (England & Wales, Scotland, or Northern Ireland). It appears on the public register. If you work from home and don't want your home address public, use a virtual office address service — widely available from £10–£30/month.
  • Step 3 — Prepare your director and shareholder details. You'll need full legal names, dates of birth, nationalities, and a service address for each director. PSC information (ownership percentages and nature of control) is also required.
  • Step 4 — Complete identity verification. Via GOV.UK One Login or an ACSP before or during the application — required for all directors and PSCs as of November 2025.
  • Step 5 — File online via Companies House or a formation agent. Go directly to the Companies House Web Incorporation Service, or use a formation agent (often faster, with bundled services). Pay the £100 digital fee.
  • Step 6 — Register with HMRC for Corporation Tax. You must do this within three months of starting to trade. Companies House and HMRC do not automatically talk to each other — this is a separate step founders regularly miss.
  • Step 7 — Open a business bank account. You legally need a separate account for your limited company. Most digital banks (Tide, Monzo Business, Starling) can open one in 24–48 hours with your Companies House number.
  • Step 8 — Consider VAT registration. If your taxable turnover exceeds or is likely to exceed £90,000 in any rolling 12-month period, VAT registration is mandatory. Below that threshold, it's optional — but voluntary registration can benefit you if your customers are VAT-registered businesses.

Can Non-UK Residents Register a UK Limited Company Online?

Yes — and it's more straightforward than many people assume. Non-UK residents can register a UK limited company online without being physically present in the UK. There is no requirement for directors or shareholders to be UK citizens or residents.

What you do need:

  • A UK registered office address. This must be a real, physical UK address — not a PO box and not outside the UK. Virtual office address services fulfil this requirement legally and typically cost £10–£50/month depending on the provider and location.
  • Identity verification. Under ECCTA, non-UK residents must still complete IDV. If GOV.UK One Login doesn't support your ID documents (it currently works best with UK and Irish documents), verification through an ACSP is your practical route.
  • A UK business bank account (eventually). Some digital banks are opening to non-residents; others require a UK address or phone number. This is often the trickiest part of the process for overseas founders — check eligibility before incorporation so you're not surprised.

Non-residents running a UK company will also need to engage a UK accountant for Corporation Tax filings and potentially for payroll if they have UK employees — factor this into your cost model.

VAT, MTD, and the Ongoing Compliance Picture

Registration is day one. What follows matters just as much. Here's the compliance landscape every new UK business owner needs to understand in 2026:

  • VAT threshold: £90,000. Unchanged since April 2024. Cross this in any rolling 12-month period and you must register for VAT — it applies whether you're a sole trader, partnership, or limited company. Registration is free via HMRC and can be done online. Once registered, you'll need to file VAT returns (usually quarterly) using Making Tax Digital-compatible accounting software.
  • Making Tax Digital for ITSA. From April 2026, sole traders and landlords with income above £50,000 must use MTD-compatible software and submit quarterly updates to HMRC — replacing the single annual Self Assessment return. The threshold drops to £30,000 in April 2027. If you're a sole trader in this income band, the administrative jump is real: quarterly filings, digital record-keeping from day one, and compatible software costs. This is a genuine structural argument for incorporating.
  • Confirmation Statement. Every limited company must file at least one Confirmation Statement per year, confirming that Companies House records are accurate. From 2026, this costs £50 per digital filing. It is not the same as your annual accounts — both are required.
  • Annual accounts. Limited companies must file statutory accounts with Companies House and a Corporation Tax return with HMRC each year. Most founders use an accountant for this — typically £500–£1,500/year for a small company, though some online accounting platforms bundle this in a monthly subscription.

Costs to Budget When Registering a UK Business in 2026

Let's put the numbers together so you're not surprised. Here's a realistic first-year cost picture for each structure:

  • Sole trader: £0 in registration fees. Annual Self Assessment filing: DIY via HMRC is free; accountant typically £150–£400/year. MTD-compatible software if required: £15–£30/month. Total government cost: £0.
  • Limited company (self-filing online): £100 incorporation fee + £50 annual Confirmation Statement = £150 in Companies House fees per year one. Add HMRC Corporation Tax registration (free), accountant fees (£500–£1,500/year), and accounting software (£20–£50/month). If you need a virtual office address, add £120–£600/year. Realistic total government + essential services: £800–£2,500 in year one.
  • Using a formation agent: Most bundle Companies House fees with registered office address, standard Memorandum & Articles, and sometimes a year of confirmation statement filing for £50–£150 total — often cheaper than self-filing when you factor in the time and the value-added services.

The rules have genuinely changed. Data from Y Combinator's portfolio, ICANN's latest reports, and registrar pricing audits all tell a more nuanced story than "just get the .com." This guide cuts through the noise so you can make a confident, financially sound decision today.

TL;DR — What Founders Need to Know Right Now

  • .com is still king but no longer the default: Only 46% of recent Y Combinator companies launched on .com, down from 100% twenty years ago — yet over 90% of $100M+ valued startups still use it, making the choice genuinely depend on your stage and ambition.
  • Brandable beats keyword every time: 87% of successful YC companies chose invented or compound names over keyword-heavy ones, and Google confirmed in 2026 that exact-match domains no longer boost rankings and can actively trigger spam filters.
  • Your registrar choice is a financial decision: The five-year cost gap between the cheapest and most expensive registrars for a single .com exceeds $44 — and GoDaddy quietly stripped consumer protections from its Terms of Service in February 2026.

The .com vs. .ai vs. .io Decision: What the Data Actually Says

The .com vs .ai vs .io for startups debate has a clear 2026 answer, and it's not the one most branding articles give you. Among Y Combinator companies — arguably the most closely watched startup cohort in the world — .com usage has fallen from 100% to just 46% over two decades. That's not a rounding error. That's a structural shift in founder behavior.

What's filling the gap? .ai has overtaken .io as the second-most-popular TLD among recent YC batches. Demand for AI-related domains has grown more than 400% over the last three years, and the average resale price for a .ai domain now exceeds $6,500 — a sign that the market takes the extension seriously. If your startup operates in artificial intelligence, machine learning, or adjacent spaces, .ai carries genuine signal. It tells investors and users something true about your business before they've read a single word of copy.

.io, meanwhile, still commands respect in developer and SaaS communities but is losing ground to .ai. It also carries a quirk worth knowing: .io is the country-code TLD for the British Indian Ocean Territory, and there have been ongoing geopolitical discussions about its long-term future. Not an imminent risk, but a real one for a domain you plan to build a brand on for a decade.

The honest framing: .com remains the safest long-term choice for a consumer-facing startup with global ambitions. But if your exact name is unavailable on .com and your category maps naturally to .ai or another extension, the stigma of non-.com is gone for most audiences under 40.

Brandable vs. Keyword Domain Names: Why Invented Names Win

The brandable vs keyword domain name question was settled years ago by SEO data, and 2026's Google update made it official. Exact-match domains — names like "best-crm-software.com" or "cheapaccounting.io" — no longer provide any ranking advantage and can now actively flag your site as potential spam in Google's classifiers. The SEO case for keyword domains is dead.

The branding case for invented names, on the other hand, is stronger than ever. Consider that 87% of Y Combinator companies chose brandable, invented, or compound names over keyword-based ones. Names like Stripe, Notion, Linear, and Vercel are memorable precisely because they don't describe — they evoke. They give the brand room to grow without boxing it into a product category that might shift.

What makes a domain name brandable? A few practical rules of thumb:

  • 4–12 characters is the sweet spot for memorability and type-ability.
  • No hyphens, no numbers — both create friction when spoken aloud and signal low-budget to experienced eyes.
  • Easy to spell after hearing it once — if you have to say "that's G-E-T-[brand]-dot-com" every time, the name is working against you.
  • Passes the radio test: could someone hear your domain name on a podcast and type it correctly? If not, reconsider.
  • No trademark conflicts — run a USPTO search before you fall in love with a name.
"The best domain name isn't the one that describes what you do. It's the one that people remember after hearing it once and can spell correctly the first time they try."

The New gTLD Reality: 1,200+ Extensions, a Handful That Matter

Here's a stat that reframes the whole conversation: 54% of startups surveyed now use a new gTLD as their primary domain, and they're achieving an 85% brand-match rate — meaning founders using extensions like .ai, .io, .co, .app, or .dev are far more likely to secure their exact desired name compared to fighting over .com inventory. That's a real operational advantage when you're naming a company.

The best domain extension for startups in 2026 depends heavily on your category:

  • .com — Still the default for consumer brands, e-commerce, and anything targeting mainstream audiences or international markets. Maximum trust, maximum resale value.
  • .ai — Earned and appropriate for AI-native products. Strong investor recognition, growing consumer familiarity. Average resale prices above $6,500 suggest real secondary market demand.
  • .io — Established in developer and SaaS communities. Slightly losing momentum to .ai but still a credible choice for technical products.
  • .co — Clean, short, and widely understood as a startup-friendly alternative. Less technical specificity than .io or .ai.
  • .app / .dev — Google-backed extensions with clear use-case signals. HTTPS is enforced by default on .app, which is a minor but real security plus.

ICANN's new gTLD application round opens in 2026 and will introduce a fresh wave of industry-specific extensions. That's worth watching, but worth calibrating your excitement: of the 1,200+ new gTLDs launched since 2014, only a small handful have built lasting audience trust. Don't anchor your brand to an extension that hasn't proven itself unless you have a compelling strategic reason.

How to Check Domain Name Availability (Without Tipping Off Domain Squatters)

Knowing how to check domain name availability sounds trivial until you realize that some registrars — and third-party WHOIS tools — have historically shared search data in ways that caused domains to be registered shortly after you looked them up. While this practice is less rampant than it was five years ago, the risk hasn't disappeared entirely.

The safest approach in 2026:

  • Use a registrar's own search tool directly — major accredited registrars (NameSilo, Cloudflare Registrar, Namecheap) have cleaner reputations on search data handling than some third-party aggregators.
  • Search in private/incognito mode to avoid browser-based tracking feeding into ad networks that domain investors monitor.
  • Don't search the same name repeatedly across multiple registrars — make your list, check once, decide fast.
  • Use ICANN's official WHOIS lookup (lookup.icann.org) for ownership information — it's neutral and doesn't have a commercial incentive to flag your search.
  • If you're researching a name that's already registered, check the expiry date — domains that are registered but point to no website often drop. Services like ExpiredDomains.net let you monitor upcoming drops.

The Real Cost of Domain Registration: Cheapest Domain Registrar for Startups in 2026

Registrar pricing is one of those founder decisions that feels small and isn't. The cheapest domain registrar for startups in 2026 isn't just about the first-year price — it's about the five-year total cost of ownership, privacy protections, and Terms of Service you're actually agreeing to.

The numbers are stark. A five-year .com registration with GoDaddy — still one of the most heavily advertised registrars — costs approximately $99.83. The same five years with NameSilo costs approximately $55.25, and NameSilo includes free WHOIS privacy. That's a $44+ difference for the same domain. Over a portfolio of domains, or over a decade of renewals, this compounds significantly.

The privacy point matters independently. WHOIS privacy should be free everywhere in 2026. Any registrar still charging for it is monetizing a basic security feature you shouldn't have to pay for. Your name, address, email, and phone number appear in public WHOIS records without privacy protection — that's not theoretical spam risk, it's guaranteed spam.

The GoDaddy ToS change in February 2026 is worth flagging specifically: GoDaddy reclassified all customers as "Business Customers," a legal maneuver that strips consumer protection rights in dispute resolution. If you're already with GoDaddy, this isn't necessarily a reason to panic-transfer today, but it's a reason to read what you're signing before renewing or registering new domains there.

Registrars worth considering for startups in 2026:

  • NameSilo — Lowest consistent renewal pricing, free WHOIS privacy, no predatory upsells.
  • Cloudflare Registrar — At-cost pricing (no markup on renewals), excellent security defaults, but requires a Cloudflare account and only supports transfers, not new registrations on some TLDs.
  • Namecheap — Competitive pricing, free WhoisGuard privacy, solid interface. Slightly higher renewal rates than NameSilo on some TLDs.
  • Porkbun — Strong pricing on newer gTLDs (.ai, .io, .app), free privacy, and a refreshingly honest pricing page.

Your website hosting choice and domain registrar don't need to be the same company — and often shouldn't be. Separating them gives you flexibility if you ever want to switch hosts without a complex domain transfer.

Domain Names as Financial Assets: The Investment Case You Didn't Know You Were Making

Here's the angle most startup domain articles skip entirely: your domain name is an appreciating or depreciating asset on your cap table, and the choice you make on day one has real financial consequences if you ever raise, sell, or pivot.

Short, memorable 4–6 letter .com domains appreciate at 12–18% annually based on secondary market data. A $10 annual renewal on a strong .com brand could represent a five-figure or six-figure asset within a decade. Domain investors know this — it's why the secondary market for premium .coms shows no signs of softening despite 1,200+ new extensions entering the market.

The inverse is also true. Most alternative TLD domains — .io, .ai, .co, and others — depreciate in resale value the moment you register them, because their value is tied almost entirely to the business built on them, not the domain itself. The exception is premium short .ai domains, where secondary market prices averaging over $6,500 suggest growing asset-class behavior. But that's for short, dictionary-word .ai names — not the startup-specific compound name you register today.

This doesn't mean "always buy .com." It means: if you're choosing between two equally good names — one on .com, one on an alternative TLD — the .com has a financial dimension the other doesn't. Factor that in, especially if you're considering spending $2,000–$5,000 on a premium .com from a domain broker versus registering a fresh name on .ai for $70.

At the acquisition stage, acquirers and investors consistently prefer .com — partly for brand trust, partly because it's a cleaner asset to transfer. If an exit is a realistic scenario within five years, that preference is worth weighing early.

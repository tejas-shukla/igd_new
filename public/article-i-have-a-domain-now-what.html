I have a domain name, now what?

J
James Okafor
Published June 2026 · 9 min read

Getting your domain registered feels like a real milestone — and it is. But most first-time founders hit the same wall immediately afterwards: now what? The domain is sitting there, doing nothing, while the clock on your idea keeps ticking.

This guide cuts straight to it. Here are the 8 moves every founder should make right after buying a domain, in the order that actually makes sense.

A domain name on its own is like buying a plot of land and leaving it empty. These eight steps turn it into a business.

The quick version — what to do in your first 48 hours

  • ✅ Enable WHOIS privacy and auto-renew (takes 2 minutes, prevents future headaches)
  • ✅ Connect your domain to a website builder
  • ✅ Set up a business email at your domain
  • ✅ Confirm HTTPS is active
  • ✅ Open a business bank account — you're now official

The remaining steps can happen across your first two weeks. Let's go through all eight.

01. Enable WHOIS privacy and auto-renew immediately

Before you do anything creative, handle two admin tasks that will save you real pain later. The moment you register a domain, both of these should be switched on.

WHOIS privacy hides your personal name, address and email from the public domain registry. Without it, your contact details are visible to anyone who looks up your domain — and spam follows within hours. Most reputable registrars (Cloudflare, Porkbun, Namecheap) include WHOIS privacy for free. If yours charges extra for it, that's worth noting when you compare next time.

Auto-renew prevents one of the most avoidable disasters in early-stage business: accidentally losing your domain. Founders lose their domains every year because a renewal email lands in spam, or a card expires. Domain speculators watch for expiring domains and can register yours within hours of it lapsing. Turn on auto-renew today and make a calendar note to check your payment method every year.

02. Connect your domain to a website builder

Your registrar sold you the domain name — the address. Now you need somewhere for that address to point: a website builder that actually hosts and serves your site to visitors.

The way the connection works: you update your domain's DNS settings (usually under "DNS Management" or "Nameservers" in your registrar account) with values your chosen website platform gives you. Most platforms make this straightforward with a step-by-step guide.

Which builder to choose depends entirely on what you're building:

  • Selling physical or digital products? Shopify handles payments, inventory and fulfilment in one place and is the clear standard for ecommerce founders.
  • SaaS, startup, or marketing site? Framer or Webflow let you build a design-led site without writing code, and both have pricing that makes sense at early stage.
  • Content or blog-first? WordPress.com gives you the most long-term flexibility and starts inexpensively, though the learning curve is steeper.
  • Just need something live fast? Wix and Squarespace are the gentlest starting points for non-technical founders who need a professional result quickly.

03. Set up a professional business email at your domain

The first email you send from hello@yourbusiness.com instead of a free Gmail address does more for your credibility than almost anything else at this stage. It signals that you are serious, that you own your brand, and that you have done the basics right.

Setting this up is straightforward. The two dominant choices for founders are:

  • Google Workspace — around £5–6 per user per month. Gives you Gmail at your domain plus Google Docs, Sheets, Meet and Drive. The natural choice if your team uses Google tools.
  • Microsoft 365 Business — slightly cheaper, starting around £4–5 per user per month. Better if your team works primarily in Word, Excel and Outlook.

Set this up before you contact your first potential customer, partner or investor. A branded email address is one of the cheapest trust signals you can buy — don't leave it on the to-do list.

04. Confirm HTTPS is active

When someone visits your site, their browser checks whether the connection is encrypted. A site without HTTPS shows a "Not secure" warning in the address bar — which immediately undermines trust, especially for anyone thinking of paying you money.

HTTPS works through an SSL certificate. The good news: if you're using any modern website builder, SSL is handled automatically when you connect your domain. You do not need to do anything technical.

The quick check: open your domain in a browser and look for a padlock icon in the address bar. If it is not there within 48 hours of connecting your domain, your website builder's support team can fix it — it is almost always a DNS propagation issue that resolves itself, but it is worth confirming.

05. Set your DNS records correctly

DNS (Domain Name System) is the internet's address book — it translates your domain name into the actual server where your site lives. When you connect to a website builder or set up business email, you are adding records to your domain's DNS.

Always copy these records exactly as your platform gives them to you. A single character wrong in a DNS record can take your site or email offline. The records you will most commonly deal with:

  • A record — points your domain to your website's server IP address.
  • CNAME record — creates an alias (e.g. making www.yourdomain.com point to the same place as yourdomain.com).
  • MX records — tell the internet where to send emails to your domain. Your email provider (Google or Microsoft) gives you these to paste in.
  • TXT records — used to verify that you own the domain with Google, email providers, and others.

DNS changes can take up to 48 hours to fully propagate globally, though in practice they usually take minutes. Do not panic if things do not appear to work immediately.

06. Open a business bank account

Having a domain and a business name makes you look like a business. Opening a business bank account makes you operate like one. This step catches more founders off-guard than any other — because it feels administrative, and there is always something more exciting to work on. But doing it early saves significant pain later.

Mixing personal and business finances is one of the most common early-stage mistakes. It creates chaos at tax time (what counts as a business expense?), makes it nearly impossible to know if you are actually profitable, and looks unprofessional to anyone who eventually scrutinises your accounts — whether that is an accountant, a co-founder, or an investor.

The modern options make this easy. In the UK, Tide and Starling both offer free business accounts that open in minutes on your phone with no minimum balance. In the US, Mercury is the standard choice for early-stage startups — no monthly fees, a clean interface, and integrations with the tools founders use. Revolut Business and Wise Business are strong picks if you will be dealing with international payments from the start.

07. Build your SEO foundations from day one

SEO is how people find your website without you spending on ads. The earlier you start, the better — because domain age and consistent content compound over time. A site that begins building authority today will rank better in six months than an identical site that only starts then.

You do not need an SEO expert to get the basics right. Here is what actually matters at this stage:

  • Write a clear page title and meta description for every page. These are what appear in search results. Your homepage title should say what you do in under 10 words.
  • Submit your site to Google Search Console. It is free and tells Google your site exists. Go to search.google.com/search-console, add your property, and verify ownership using a TXT record in your DNS settings.
  • Submit your sitemap. Most website builders generate one automatically at yourdomain.com/sitemap.xml. Add it in Search Console so Google indexes your pages faster.
  • Get your first external links. A listing on a relevant directory, a mention in a community post, or a link from a partner website all signal to Google that you are real and worth showing to people. Aim for two or three before you launch.
  • Make your homepage say what you do clearly. Vague hero text ("Empowering your journey") does not help with SEO or with the human beings who land on your page.

08. Set up the rest of your founder stack

A domain is the first brick, but the founder who launches fastest is the one who treats the full setup as a connected system rather than a list of separate tasks to handle someday. Here is what comes after a domain and website:

  • Accounting software — track income and expenses from the very first transaction. Wave is free and more than sufficient at early stage. Xero and FreeAgent are the natural upgrades once you have growing transaction volume or need to handle payroll and VAT.
  • Payment processing — decide how you will take money before your first sale, not during it. Stripe is the developer favourite with clear pricing. Paddle acts as a merchant of record, which dramatically simplifies international tax for SaaS founders selling to customers in multiple countries.
  • Marketing and analytics — install PostHog or Google Analytics before your first visitor arrives. Set up a basic email capture (even a simple form linking to Mailchimp) so you start building an audience before you launch, not after.

None of these need to be perfect immediately. But each one is significantly harder to set up retrospectively when you have customers and revenue moving through your business.

The order matters more than the speed

Getting your domain was step one. The logical sequence from here is: website → email → banking → accounting → payments → marketing. Each step in this chain depends on the one before it. A website builder needs a domain. Business email needs a domain. Banking needs you to exist as a business. Accounting needs a bank account to connect to.

Most founders treat these as separate, occasional tasks picked up in whatever order feels urgent. The founders who launch fastest treat them as one connected checklist — because they are.
